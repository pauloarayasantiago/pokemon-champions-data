# 11 Completely Redesigned Moves

**Channel:** Wavering Eyes | **Date:** unknown | **Views:** 85,042 | **Duration:** 18:21

---

Over the years, some Pokémon moves have changed. We've seen buffs, nerfs, and even moves that were simply removed from the game.  However, the moves I'd like to talk about in this video are ones that have been reworked or reworked to the point where they're virtually unrecognizable compared to how they functioned in the debut games.  The names of the moves haven't changed, but their actual usefulness and reasons for using them in combat have.  And some of these changes are not subtle.  To better clarify, let's start with an example of something that doesn't fall under what I'm talking about. The power of Life Drain increased from 20 to 80. This was undoubtedly a huge boost to the move, but the actual theme of Life Drain as a move didn't really change.  This is still a move designed to deal damage and steal health. So it doesn't quite fit the description of what I'm going to talk about.  Now, I think you could argue that the amplification is a redesign in itself.  This is definitely a change.  But believe it or not, there are a few tricks that have been modified in a much stranger way.  So, in no particular order, let's get to it.  The low kick is a unique fighting technique that you are probably familiar with. Its power level depends on the target's weight, reaching 120 power for any Pokémon weighing 200 kg or more.  So, against tough enemies, it's essentially close combat with no drawbacks. However, in Generations 1 and 2, he did something completely different. This was previously a 50 strength combat move that had a 30% chance to shudder.  It also had 90% accuracy. In fact, it was a completely different technique, but it was indeed a Low Kick. Sure, it's a little strange to see shudder text for this trick, but that's exactly what he's done before.    It wasn't until Generation 3 that it became the weight-based technique we know it as today.  Its accuracy has also been increased to 100%, and the chance to flinch has, of course, completely disappeared.  This was undoubtedly one of the greatest transformations any reception has ever undergone. Interestingly, weight actually existed in the first generation, but only as specific text in Pokedex entries.  It didn't intersect with the combat mechanics until the third generation. Next we have the Hyper Beam, another first- generation move that is often portrayed as a sort of ultimate move .  There's no denying that it's powerful, but the drawback of needing an entire turn to reload makes this technique difficult to justify its full use .  You also can't switch, which of course makes you vulnerable to incoming attacks.  All of this is true if we're talking about the second-generation Hyper Beam.  In the first generation, the cooldown could be completely bypassed if the Hyper Beam was knocked out.  And since the Hyper Beam is so powerful, it obviously had the potential to do so. So this actually came up often .  Tauros, who is a perfect storm of first- generation nonsense, absolutely loved this trick.  It got all the benefits of the first generation weirdness and none of the drawbacks, and that extended to the abuse of the Hyperbeam. Of course, they fixed that by moving to the Gold and Silver versions.  Now it just functions as intended. Perhaps this is not so much a redesign as such, but an oversight, but in any case, the first- generation Hyperbeam was unique, to say the least, in that it had a different functionality and purpose for its use.    The next technique I want to talk about is Teleport.  This is one of the strangest techniques , because for the first 20 years of Pokémon games, it had no use in combat. In fact, choosing Teleport in a trainer battle actually resulted in the reception failing.  If you used it during an encounter with a wild Pokémon, you would flee the battle.  A more common version of this interaction, and probably the one people associate this whole trick with, is the reverse, where a wild Abra appears and teleports away on the first turn because that's the only trick it learns.  This was clearly just a move focused on the RPG elements of the series until it was reworked into Let's Go Pikachu and Eevee.  So, how has Teleport changed?  Well , they turned it into a toggle movement, which I thought was a pretty intuitive solution for a movement that was probably on the verge of collapse.  However, there is more to it than just switching. It's a negative priority, minus six negative priorities, to be exact.  So, Teleport is almost guaranteed to go last.  If you're just admiring competitive Pokémon from afar, you might wonder how it can even be good?  Switching is obviously a common occurrence in competitive Pokémon , but you can just do it manually. Also, why not just use U-Turn or Farewell Shot? What benefit can there be from switching negative priority?  Well, none on Abra.  That would be bad.  The entire Abra line is simply too fragile to willingly take a hit.  The basic idea is that you have a bulky support Pokemon, like Bliss, use Teleport, and then guarantee it gets hit because of this negative priority.  which then allows you to turn around and send out a Pokemon that might not have been able to withstand the blow Bliss just dealt, but could be just the Pokemon to actually get the knockout.  This naturally pairs well with the Wish move, as it allows you to safely turn around and heal an ally.    The 1st generation wrap is even better than you think.    It's pretty well known by now that in Red and Blue, Wrap is a multi-turn move that immobilizes your opponent for two to five turns, blocking their ability to choose a move, but there's more to it than that .  I'm also going to add Bind, Fire Spin, and Clamp here, as they all function the same and were also introduced in Generation 1.  In addition to preventing your opponent from moving, they will also take damage each turn equivalent to the amount the first Wrap hit dealt.  If the first hit was critical, then it will be two to five turns of critical damage.  This is not how it works in later generations , where the linked Pokémon gains 1/16 of its maximum HP each turn.  Dragonite, which has the highest attack stat in Generation 1, takes full advantage of this.  Yes , the base power of the Wrap is only 15, but that's not all when you can use it over and over again. The attack is still ongoing. Not to mention that critical strike chances in Gen 1 depend on speed.  So, a Dragonite with a base speed of 80 has approximately a 15% chance of a critical hit. Speaking of speed, if Dragonite or any Wrapper user is faster than your opponent in Generation 1, you can win right away if you don't miss, because you'll just prevent your opponent from making their move.  The move they choose will never happen if the Wrapper goes first. Dragonite even has access to Agility and Thunder Wave, so he has the tools to stay ahead of everything. The wrapper also has a funny glitch associated with it. Since this is a multi-turn move in Generation 1, only one power point is used for each Wrap cycle. Keep this in mind. One difference with the 1st generation Wrapper that I have n't mentioned yet is that it doesn't catch opponent's Pokémon like it does in any other generation. They can switch freely, they just can't attack. So, if the linked Pokémon switches during a two-to -five turn cycle, the Dragonite, which is still locked in motion, will actually do another Wrap on the incoming Pokémon, consuming another movement PP.  In the event that Dragonite uses up its last Wrap PP, and at some point during that last cycle the opponent's Pokémon switches, Dragonite will still Wrap the newly switched Pokémon, despite it having zero PP.  As a result, that zero becomes 63. So, yes, you now have 63 Wrap power points instead of zero.  There is another similar glitch that can occur, related to the interaction of the Wrap with the Hyper Beam. If Wrap misses during the Hyper Beam cooldown turn and the Wrap user is faster, the Pokémon with Hyper Beam will effectively just use Hyper Beam again instead of cooldown. And this can cause the same power point glitch .  Okay, I think you get the point.  In the first generation, the "Wrap" movement was non-functional, so it had to be redesigned.  The solution they came up with later was to have it deal two to five turns of passive damage, just like before, but now both Pokémon can act and the linked Pokémon can't switch.  I think this is a healthy and thematically appropriate change for these types of movements.  They're obviously not as good as their first-generation predecessors, but how do you actually fix a movement like that?    There have been several other notable changes to these types of movements in recent years.  In Generation V, an item called Band-Aid was added , which, when held by a Wrapper user, doubles the residual damage dealt to a bound Pokémon, from 1/16 to 1/8 of its maximum HP. Then in the sixth generation they actually just increased the damage to 1/8 without "Band Link".  With "Band-Connection" it now becomes 1/6.  That's all with the connecting movements.  This is undoubtedly another case where the modern version of a trick is no different from how it worked in the debut game.  Chatter is infamous as the first and only Pokémon Go catch to implement a microphone feature on the DS.  You could say something into your DS, and after selecting Chatter in battle, it would play a very distorted version of the recording.  It wasn't necessary, you could still use the technique, but it would n't be as good.  By default, without a note attached , Chatter dealt damage and had a 1% chance to confuse the target.  If you were recording something, the chance of getting confused was 1%, 11%, or 31% depending on the volume of the recording.  Essentially, the louder you yelled into your DS, the more likely you were to confuse the target.  If that seems strange, that's how it was, especially looking back on it years later.  But at the time it seemed like something normal. It seemed like it was just another thing coded by Nintendo, coexisting with Nintendo's other experimental ideas at the time.  They weakened it significantly in 5th generation, requiring audio data to be present on your Chatbot for this chance of secondary confusion to be possible at all. Additionally, the actual chance of confusion was reduced to 10%.  This was a strange and almost accidental nerf that I doubt many people know about. It seems even stranger considering that this technique was quickly updated and strengthened in the 6th generation to always confuse the target.  They also made it 65 power instead of 60. You can no longer record audio data for Chatter, and I think the purpose of choosing this trick is different now. He went from being tricky in a fun, gambling way to being tricky in a more annoying way. This is one of those moves that, if it were on a really good Pokémon, would be talked about a lot more.  "Sliding Stone" didn't always flinch.  In the 1st generation, it was just a stone trick that dealt damage.  It and " Rock Throw" were really the only rock moves in the game, so it's understandable that there wasn't much emphasis on making these moves unique.  Being a stone type movement in the 1st generation was inherently unique because of this.  " Stone Type Throw" is similar to a later version of " Stone Type Throw" .  Also, while researching this, I learned that " Stone Type Throw" had 65% accuracy in 1st generation.  Wow.  In any case, the 30% chance to flinch appeared in Generation 2, and that's how Stone-type Throw functions to this day. However, there is another feature of this movement.  With the introduction of dual battles in Generation 3, Game Freak was tasked with classifying all existing moves as single targets, dual targets, or moves that hit the entire field, including allies.  " Stone-type throw" has, of course, become a move that hits both opponents with that 30 % chance to flinch. So if you hit both opponents with a "Stone-type Throw", you're more likely to flinch than not. If you've ever encountered Pokémon Champions , you know that this little design choice, made back in Generation 2, is more relevant than ever.  The outcome of a battle being turned upside down by a shudder from Stone Descent is a fairly common occurrence in the current metagame, whether you like it or not.  By the way, about the "shudder" technique, let's talk about "Waterfall".  In my opinion, this is a trick with a pretty cool comeback story.    A common and admittedly easy misconception about Waterfall is that it debuted in Generation II and was mostly just a main Pokémon. In fact, this is not true. This was a first- generation technique that was Goldin and Seeking's signature technique. No other Pokémon could learn it. This, like many things in the first generation, was little known and most likely just for the sake of brevity.  It's kind of a fun award for anyone who taught Goldin.  In the first- third generations, Waterfall had no chance to flinch, and it was a pre-physical special split. So, as a water trick, it was always special.  Given all of this, there was simply no reason to use it instead of surfing at all.  That all changed with the release of Diamond and Pearl, who turned Waterfall into a physical move and also gave it a 20% chance to stagger.  So , seemingly overnight, Waterfall became not just a viable, but the best physical water move in the game, and Gyarados still uses it as his primary water move.  What I like most about this change is that it makes more sense when you look at Seaking's specs. He was always going to be a physical attacker. So now he can use his signature technique to his heart 's content.  Next, I'm going to talk about Rear and Whirlwind together, as they're mostly the same technique, but with a few minor generational differences here and there .  As we know them today, these are important phase moves that force the opponent to switch to another Pokémon and bring in a new one. Their most notable use is to push Boost Sweeper to reset its stats .  It's a really elegant solution that helps balance the game so that when certain Pokémon are able to boost their stats and threaten to overwhelm you, the battle doesn't end on the spot, especially when you know what they're up to.  Another use for the roar/ whirlwind is to use them in conjunction with obstacles such as spikes and hidden stone.    It's not at all uncommon for this to be a legal win condition in many single-player formats.  Now you might ask why not just do it with a fast Pokemon so you can go first and prevent your opponent from acting over and over again, slowly damaging them until their HP reaches zero. Well, they made phase moves priority minus six, which prevents exactly this scenario. Regardless of the state of the game, you can practically guarantee that these moves will be the last.  Okay, now that that's over, let's talk about how these movements have changed.  In the first generation, Roar and Whirlwind actually had neutral priority, but these moves would actually fail if used in a trainer battle.  The only thing they could do was force the savage battle to an end. In this respect, they were something close to teleportation.  In the second generation, Roar/Whirlwind gained the phasing effect I talked about for trainer battles, as well as negative priority to ensure they couldn't go first.  One weird little feature of Whirlwind in Generation 2 is that it hits Pokémon that are in a semi-invulnerable flight turn.  I think it's cool when movements have these unwritten but intuitive effects.  However, it is surprising that this was only for one generation. Another notable change that Roar/Whirlwind received was the bypassing of Protection, Detection, and Barbed Shield.  This was back in the 6th generation, and this is how these movements function to this day.  Destruction is a move I almost included on this list because I initially thought it was more of a buff than a redesign.  I've come to the conclusion that it's both, so let's delve into the history of what is arguably the best dark movement of all time , and how it's changed. When Destruction was introduced in Generation 3 , its power was only 20, but the item removal aspect was the only reason to use it.  In older generations, you never really used this technique to deal damage. It is primarily about removing objects.  The damage from the chips is like a small bonus, and that's it .  It may not seem like a great thing, but being able to remove valuable items like Remnants very early in a fight is extremely powerful. Fast forward to the 6th generation, and this technique has been enhanced like no other technique before.  His strength has increased from 20 to 65, but that's not all.  If you knock down the item, which you most likely will, the move gets a 1.5x power boost, essentially making it 97 power. This is not just the best Dark-type move, but one of the best moves in all of Pokémon.  The reason for pressing this technique has changed from simply removing an item to a really strong dark technique that also removes an item. Dark, as an offensive type , has become increasingly popular over time, and "Destroy" in modern Pokémon is a move that any physical attacker would be willing to kill for access to. "Dizzying Strike " is a common technique introduced in the first generation, only learned by Kangaskhan at the time, and it had no side effects.  The 20% chance to confuse was not added to the second generation. You might be surprised to learn that the move called the Dizzy Blow didn't always have such a chance of confusing.  I don't know if they thought Dizzy Blow would be too powerful or annoying to also mess with in the first generation, or if maybe they just forgot to add it, but it appeared in the gold and silver versions.  The 20% chance to confuse came at the perfect time, as stiff competition emerged in the form of "Rebound" and "Super Speed."  These are just definitely better normal moves than "Dizzy Blow ", but they don't have a chance to confuse, so "Dizzy Blow " has it.  Over the generations, a small handful of other Pokémon have also received this move, but no other changes have been made to it .  If you think about it, this is a perfect move for Spinda. And last but not least : we have a signature technique called Porygon-Conversion.  In the first generation, it copies the opponent's type.  If your opponent's Pokémon has a dual type, then you also get that dual type.  This is a pretty cool concept because many types resist themselves, so it's an interesting counterplay for Porygon.  It seems simple enough, but Game Freak decided to completely change this technique. As in "Gold and Silver", Conversion changes the user's type to match the type of one of their own techniques, chosen randomly.  I'm not sure if this is better or worse than how it worked in the first generation. I think it depends on the situation.  But yeah, it's really weird because they also made Conversion 2, the sequel, in the same generation.  As for Conversion 1, this move will undergo another change in the next generation .  Starting with Gen 6, Conversion will change the user's typing type to match the type occupying the first turn slot.  This has its advantages, because now you can be sure what type you will turn into.  This is a rare case where setting your move slot has a real impact on combat , beyond just preference.
