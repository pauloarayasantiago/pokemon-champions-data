# How the New Pokemon Champions Training System Changes EVERYTHING

**Channel:** DragonWhale | **Date:** unknown | **Views:** 3,685 | **Duration:** 14:15

---

In the recent Pokemon Direct, we finally had a good look at several new features that will be in Pokemon Champions, a game scheduled to release next year. Prior to the Direct, we knew pretty much nothing aside from the return of Mega Evolutions and Aegislash, so we received a lot of new information about what we can expect from the game. The feature that was the most impactful by far and has the entire world of competitive Pokemon celebrating is the new training feature. Today, in this video, I will be taking a deep dive into the system, what it means, and how it changes competitive Pokemon forever. So the showcase of the feature starts off with the Gardevoir that the players scouted into their possession using the recruiting feature of the game. The first thing we notice is that we have a brand new stat point system in which each Pokemon has access to 66 points. This Gardevoir currently uses all 66 points, with 32 in HP, 32 in special attack, and 2 in special defense, as well as a modest nature indicated by the upwards arrows in special attack and downwards arrows in attack. One thing that's worth noting is that natures are no longer called natures and are instead referred to as stat alignments. This is likely due to the fact that Pokemon champions made this element easily modifiable, and easily altering the nature of the Pokemon probably raised some concerns over at Game Freak HQ. Anyway, in order to determine the meaning behind the new stat point system, we will be looking at the EVs and IVs that are necessary for Gardevoir to have the same identical stats in previous games. For those of you who don't know about EVs and IVs, here is a 60 second explanation of what they are. IVs are short for individual values, maxed out at 31 per stat, and are static from the moment the Pokemon is born. However, since Pokemon Sun and Moon, it can be maxed to 31 by talking to a hyper-training NPC and using bottle caps. Think of it as the talent of the Pokemon. EVs are short for effort values, maxed out at 252 per stat, and the total EVs across all stats cannot exceed 510. All Pokemon start with zero EVs, but EVs can be increased and decreased by various methods. EVs and IVs are both important, as they both add points to the stat. IVs increase the stat by 0.5 for every point, and EVs increase the stat by 0.5 for every 4 points, and a stat will round down if it has an extra 0.5 points. Now that we have the basics of EVs and IVs out of the way, let's get back to the Gardevoir. Plugging the values into Pokemon Showdown, we see that the Gardevoir has 31 IVs in all stats, as well as the maximum 252 EVs in HP and Special Attack, as well as 12 in Special Defense. From this, we can deduce that the 32 points in HP and Special Attack is indicative of the 252 EVs in older games, and the 2 points in Special Defense represents 12 EVs. But why is the limit of the new stat system 32 points per stat? Why not keep it 252, like in the old games? To answer this question, we need to remember that EVs give 0.5 stats for every 4 EVs, and IVs give 0.5 for every IV. As such, at 252 EVs, the bonus given from EVs is 31.5, which is pretty close to the 32 value. But we also must remember that the 31 IVs is giving us 15.5 bonus stats as well, which means that the first 4 EVs, which add 0.5 to the stat, is enough to push it to the next integer threshold of 16. Therefore, the maximum bonus given to the stat through EVs is effectively 32, which is identical to the limit of the new stat point system introduced in Pokemon Champions. In other words, the stat point system is literally the amount of bonus stats that are given to the Pokemon through what used to be the EV system in older games. This is a good change as it greatly simplifies the frankly archaic system of EVs to make it a lot easier for newer players to comprehend. However, there is something wrong about the EV spread that we plugged into the Gardevoir to match the one in Pokemon Champions. If you look at the Pokemon Showdown screen, the number of remaining EVs is minus 6 and bolded in red, and that's because the total EVs that this Gardevoir has is 516, which is 6 over the 510 limit. In fact, because EVs are only viable in multiples of 4 and 510 isn't divisible by 4, there's only 508 EVs that are effective on a Pokemon. Basically, this Gardevoir in Pokemon Champions has 8 more effective EVs than the Gardevoirs in previous games, which is worth a whole entire stat. People who are familiar with the common EV spread of 252, 252, 4 also might have immediately noticed the increase of 8 EVs for the Gardevoir in Pokemon Champions. So if that's the case, does this mean that Pokemon in Pokemon Champions are going to be slightly stronger than in previous games? Why did they give us 66 stat points instead of 65, which should have limited the Gardevoir to the usual 252, 252, 4 like in previous games? The answer to this question lies in the first 4 EV points of a stat. Like I mentioned before, the first 4 EVs on a stat is enough to push the stat to the next integer threshold, thanks to the extra 0.5 from the IV bonus points. This has led some competitive players to reduce 8 EVs from a stat to put 4 EVs into 2 empty stats, which removes 1 point from this first stat, but gains 1 point into 2 other stats, netting an extra stat point in total. One example of an EV spread of this nature is 252, 244, 444. When we insert that into the new stat point system, we will end up with 32, 31, 1, 1, 1, which adds up to 66. In other words, Pokemon that have invested in 5 different stats will not gain any stat benefits from the new stat point system, but Pokemon that have 3 will have an extra stat point available. It makes sense considering that Pokemon with 5 stats were netting an extra stat point with the EV system, so this pretty much just even things out. Personally, this new stat point system is a fantastic change, because it simplifies the stat system dramatically and removes sub-optimization mistakes that can come from the EV system. With EVs, if you don't distribute your EVs as an odd multiple of 4, you are effectively losing out on valuable EV points. This also has the added side effect of making investments into 3 or 5 stats the only viable option. If you invest in only 4 stats, you will end up with an extra 4 EV points that only have merit when put into a 5th empty stat. With the stat point system, not only is the optimization of points no longer a concern, it also becomes viable to invest in 4 stats, and in some rare cases, all 6 stats. This is also the first time that a Pokemon game displays these values directly to the player, despite being one of the most important fundamental aspects in competitive Pokemon. In previous games, the closest they got to this was a graph, with no numbers to indicate anything whatsoever. There were no explanations for how to increase EVs and how much they increased it by, and the only resources available regarding it were through 3rd party fansites. It honestly felt like Pokemon were trying everything not to acknowledge the existence of EVs, despite being a requirement to learn about competitive Pokemon. While keeping things under the hood was somewhat common for competitive video games in the 90s, this mentality was frankly unacceptable for a competitive game in 2025, so I'm glad that Pokemon Champions is rectifying this with the new training system. The biggest improvement to the training system aside from the swap to stat points is the speed in which stats are also able to be distributed. In the showcase, the Gardevoir transferred all of its HP stat points to speed in roughly 10 seconds, which is much faster than what we had to do when redistributing EVs in old games. In Pokemon Scarlet and Violet, we needed to gather different kinds of resources, carefully remove or completely reset the EVs on a Pokemon, and carefully re-add EVs to the desired stats, amounting to around 10-20 minutes depending on how many resources you needed to obtain to begin with. Note that this is the most recent games where the system has already undergone several iterations of improvements from the past. The further back you go, the more arduous the task of EV training becomes, even reaching over an hour to fully EV train a Pokemon in the first games with the EV system. In Pokemon Champions, this is thankfully fixed with the quick redistribution and the VP currency being the only resource that is necessary. Setting EVs to the side, I am personally a fan of how Pokemon Champions handled the IV system as well. IVs, like I mentioned earlier, is the innate talent value of the Pokemon, and in previous games, it has a value between 0 and 31 for each stat. However, Pokemon Champions seems to have scrapped the concept completely and gave all Pokemon Max 31s across the board, or in other words, perfect Pokemon. Pokemon's IV system has long been an infamous element in the competitive scene, as it requires grinding for perfection that is random for every Pokemon. It is one of the primary reasons for people hacking, or gen'ing, Pokemon by using external tools to generate perfect Pokemon into the game with the desired IVs. While the new bottle cap system in later games let players perfect the Pokemon's IVs after obtaining them, it still required time and effort, as well as lacking the ability to reduce the Pokemon's IVs to 0. Maximizing all IVs by default and making them unmodifiable simplifies the stat system and also reduces a lot of headaches. The EV and IV system being replaced by this new training system is a very good step in the right direction, as it lowers the barrier to entry significantly and removes a lot of negative aspects that the old system has had. However, it is important to note that the new system, in particular the stat point system, only works with level 50 Pokemon. When the Pokemon is level 100, the EVs raise the stats of a Pokemon by 127, which is way more than 66. However, if you take a look at the training screen, or the box screen, or even the team preview screen, the levels of the Pokemon aren't displayed anywhere. From these two observations, it is highly likely that there is no such thing as levels in Pokemon Champions. People who play VGC or BSS aren't really affected by this, as both player bases have been playing with the level 50 lock for almost a decade now. But it does affect Smogon players, whose formats often play with level 100 Pokemon. The difference between level 100 and level 50 in and of itself isn't that significant, with damage ranges being slightly higher for level 50s. Personally, from a beginner friendliness perspective, level 50 lock is a much easier thing to get accustomed to regarding the Pokemon's stats. With the levels being locked at level 50 and IVs being maxed out, the stat is going to be equal to the base stat plus 20 plus the stat points, or 75 plus stat points in the case of HP. And then you just simply add or subtract the nature modifier. Really easy stuff. So anyway, I'm a fan of the removal of levels. Trimming the excess fat to make competitive Pokemon even more accessible to a wider audience is something that I will always welcome. Pokemon Champions also makes the game more accessible by changing the way they teach moves to a Pokemon. Previously, you had to level up, evolve, use TMs, or go to certain NPCs to teach your Pokemon your desired moves. But now, it can all be done by selecting it from a list. Here, we see a Gardevoir change its Expanding Force to Psychic. But I want you to see how in-depth the move descriptions are in this game compared to the old games. Expanding Force explicitly states the 50% damage modifier in Psychic Terrain. It tells you that the chances for the special defense drop is 10%. There's no fluff, straight to the point, and there's no ambiguity. This is another great step in the right direction for newcomers to learn more about competitive Pokemon. Another aspect regarding moves that I'm personally fond of is the removal of PP maxing. In previous Pokemon games, players had the option to increase the move's PP by 60% using a PP max. It is especially relevant for moves that only have 5 PP as it increased the number to 8, and these moves tend to be very good. The most notable example of this is recovery moves. Simply lowering the number of times it can be used for 8 to 5 greatly limits defensive Pokemon. With the influx of new players that are expected to come with Pokemon champions, I think it's a good thing to limit more defensive Pokemon and create faster gameplay to keep them interested in the game. The final element in the training screen is the ability, which seems to be easily modifiable as the other things that we covered already, and everything is done through the VP currency. We can see that it costs 2 VP for each stat point, 100 VP for every move, 200 VP for a new nature, I mean, a stat alignment, and 400 VP to change the ability of the Pokemon. That means the maximum possible cost of training a Pokemon is 1,132 VP, and 2,132 VP if you include the 1,000 VP it costs to recruit that Pokemon as well. The showcase told us that the only way to obtain VP in the game is to play ranked battles, so depending on how much we get for every victory, it will determine how easy it is for players to train their Pokemon. Regardless, all of these changes are positives for the competitive Pokemon community, and every aspect is contributing to lowering the very high barrier to entry problem that plagued this community for as long as it existed.
